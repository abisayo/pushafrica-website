Poverty

The World Bank says poverty rate in Nigeria has increased to 46 percent in 2023

Nearly half the population now lives below the national poverty line. The response that lasts is not relief — it is capability.

PUSH Africa Editorial14 September 2023 · 4 min read

Nigeria's poverty rate has climbed to 46 percent, according to World Bank estimates — a figure that represents tens of millions of people whose daily reality is defined by scarcity, insecurity and limited opportunity.

Behind the headline is a pattern we see every day in our work: poverty is rarely caused by a lack of effort. It is caused by a lack of access — to skills, to markets, to capital, and to the networks that convert effort into income.

Why the numbers keep rising

Population growth, inflation and underemployment compound one another. Each year, large numbers of young people enter the labour market, while the number of secure, well-paying roles grows far more slowly.

The result is a widening gap between people who can access opportunity and those who cannot. Closing it requires more than short-term transfers.

Giving to those in need what they could be gaining from their own initiative may well be the kindest way to destroy people.

The case for skills

Vocational and technical education remains one of the most durable anti-poverty interventions available. A trained welder, developer, caregiver or farmer does not just earn today — they hold an asset that appreciates with experience.

That is why we invest in four schools and a set of programmes designed to move people from vulnerability to self-reliance:

  • T-VET training that is hands-on, industry-relevant and certified.
  • Enterprise support for those who want to create jobs, not just take them.
  • Agricultural development for rural communities where most poverty is concentrated.
  • Advocacy for pro-poor policies that make these pathways possible at scale.

What must change

No single organisation can reverse a 46 percent poverty rate. But the direction of travel is clearer than it has ever been, and three shifts matter most:

  1. Invest in employability, not just enrolment. Training must end in work, income or enterprise.
  2. Connect skills to markets. A qualification is only valuable if there is demand for it.
  3. Measure outcomes honestly. Progress should be verifiable, not asserted.

These are the principles behind every programme we run — and the reason we publish our results openly.
